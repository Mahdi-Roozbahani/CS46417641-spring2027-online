Course Overview #

Important
CSE students should note that CS 7641 is not allowed as a substitute for the CSE core course CSE 6740, and that they cannot get credit for both CSE 6740 and CS 7641.

Course Description #

This course introduces techniques in machine learning with an emphasis on algorithms and their applications to real-world data. We will investigate the following question: how to computationally extract useful knowledge from data for decision making and task support! The course will also cover briefly Ethics in Machine Learning and Secure Computing. We will focus on machine learning methods, which are organized into three main course objectives:

  1. Basic math for data science and machine learning
    • Linear algebra
    • Probability and statistics
    • Information theory
    • Optimization
  2. Unsupervised machine learning for data exploration
    • Clustering analysis
    • Dimensionality reduction
    • Kernel density estimation
  3. Supervised learning for predictive data analysis
    • Tree-based models
    • Support vector machines
    • Linear classification and regression
    • Neural networks

Prerequisites for this course include (1) basic knowledge of probability, statistics, and linear algebra; (2) basic programming experience in Python.

Course Objectives and Learning Outcomes #

  • Introduce to you the pipeline of Machine Learning
  • Help you understand major machine learningalgorithms
  • Help you learn to apply tools for real dataanalysis problems
  • Encourage you to do research in data science andmachine learning

In addition to the technical content, this class includes the following learning objectives:

  • Structuring a task into a machine learning work flow
  • Collaborating effectively in a remote learning environment
  • Conducting peer evaluation in a constructive format
  • Communicating technical content in a concise and effective manner

General Course Policies #

Online and Asynchronous Participation #

All core lectures are pre-recorded. There is no required live lecture attendance. Students are responsible for watching the assigned videos, studying the lecture notes, completing assessments within their availability windows, and participating in the team project.

Because this is an asynchronous course with students in many time zones, routine deadlines use Anywhere on Earth so every student can use the full listed calendar date.

Official Platforms and Communication #

Ed Discussion #

Ed Discussion is the official location for announcements, class-wide clarifications, question-and-answer threads, office-hour notices, and private messages to the teaching team. Students must enable notifications and check Ed regularly.

Canvas #

Canvas is the official location for lecture modules, quizzes, exams, project submissions, approved reference-sheet uploads, and the gradebook. Canvas timestamps determine whether a submission was received before a deadline.

Gradescope #

Gradescope is used only for the ungraded problem sets. A displayed score or automated result on Gradescope is diagnostic feedback and does not contribute to the course grade.

Anywhere on Earth Deadlines #

A due date means 23:59 Anywhere on Earth, abbreviated AoE, unless an official Ed Discussion announcement states a course-wide exception. AoE is UTC-12. It allows every student, regardless of location, to use the full listed calendar date. Canvas may display the equivalent deadline in the student’s local time zone.

Students should convert AoE to their local time well before a deadline. Time-zone misunderstanding, daylight-saving changes, travel, work schedules, or an incorrect personal-calendar conversion do not create an automatic extension.

Assessment Independence #

Quizzes and exams must be completed independently. Students may not communicate with another person, use generative AI, search the web, consult unauthorized notes, use a second device, or obtain assistance during a quiz or exam.

The project has different rules. Collaboration within the registered team and disclosed use of generative AI are allowed, subject to the project requirements.

Course Materials and Problem Sets #

Lecture videos, lecture notes, quiz questions, exam questions, problem sets, and course-created code are provided for enrolled students. Note that the problem sets (homeworks) solutions will not be avaialble and students are responsible to use Gradescope auto grader and benefit from ML instructional team members to solve them and understand them. Students may not post, sell, upload, transmit, or redistribute these materials outside the course without written permission.

The four problem sets are optional and ungraded. Students may discuss concepts from them on Ed Discussion at the whiteboard level, but they may not publicly post complete course solutions or distribute official solution materials.

Online Office Hours #

Office hours are optional, online, and designed to provide focused help without requiring students to reserve spreadsheet time slots.

How an Office-Hour Session Works #

  1. A TA posts an Ed thread announcing that the office-hour session has started. The title will identify the TA, date, time, and general topic if the session has a focus.
  2. Students who need help reply to that thread with a short description of the question. Do not post private information, grades, complete code, or confidential project data.
  3. The TA may answer a question directly in the public thread when the answer is useful to the class.
  4. When a private or screen-sharing conversation is appropriate, the TA sends the student or project team a private Ed message and shares a private Zoom\Teams link there.
  5. The TA works through the queue as time allows.
  6. At the end of the scheduled period, the TA posts that the office-hour session has ended.

Do not join another student’s private Zoom\Teams meeting unless the TA invites you. Project teammates may join the same private meeting when the question concerns their shared project.

Why This Process Is Used #

This model allows the teaching team to:

  • Help several students through one public explanation when appropriate.
  • Move sensitive or individualized questions into a private conversation.
  • Avoid publicly posting reusable Zoom links.
  • Reduce unused reservation slots and scheduling administration.
  • Serve students in multiple time zones through a rotating office-hour schedule.

Student Responsibilities #

  • Arrive with a specific question.
  • Identify the relevant lecture, problem-set question, code fragment, error message, or project requirement.
  • Explain what you already tried.
  • Remove sensitive data before screen sharing.
  • Be prepared to share a minimal reproducible example rather than an entire codebase.
  • Understand that joining the queue does not guarantee service before the session ends.

Scope of Office-Hour Help #

TAs may:

  • Explain course concepts.
  • Clarify problem-set feedback.
  • Discuss a small code example or isolated error.
  • Clarify project requirements and evaluation design.
  • Discuss team-process concerns privately.

TAs will not:

  • Debug a complete assignment or project repository.
  • Choose the entire project approach for a team.
  • Review every line of a report before submission.
  • Provide solutions to active quizzes or exams.
  • Guarantee that a proposed model, experiment, or bonus request will earn a particular score.

Project Meetings Are Separate #

The semester project has exactly two required TA interactions: the graded progress meeting and the graded final presentation. Optional office hours do not replace either required interaction and do not add additional required mentor meetings.

Questions the Teaching Team Can and Cannot Answer #

Good questions #

  • “I understand the definition of conditional independence, but I do not see why this factorization follows.”
  • “The problem-set feedback says my numerical value is incorrect. Can you explain which assumption I may have applied incorrectly?”
  • “Our project metric is unstable because the positive class is rare. Would precision-recall evaluation be more appropriate than accuracy?”
  • “The project page and Canvas appear to show different dates. Which one controls?”

Questions that are too broad #

  • “Can you debug my code?”
  • “Can you tell me exactly what model our project should use?”
  • “Can you review our entire report before we submit it?”
  • “Can you tell me what will be on the exam?”

Anywhere on Earth Deadlines #

Unless an official Ed Discussion announcement states otherwise, assessment deadlines identified as AoE are due at 23:59 Anywhere on Earth, abbreviated AoE, on the listed calendar date.

AoE is equivalent to UTC−12. It allows students throughout the world to use the full listed calendar date.

For example, a deadline of Monday at 23:59 AoE corresponds to:

  • Tuesday at 7:59 a.m. Eastern Daylight Time, EDT, when Atlanta is observing daylight-saving time.
  • Tuesday at 6:59 a.m. Eastern Standard Time, EST, when Atlanta is observing standard time.

Therefore, a Monday AoE deadline will normally appear as Tuesday morning for students in Atlanta and elsewhere in the Eastern Time Zone.

Canvas may display the equivalent deadline in the time zone configured in the student’s Canvas account. Students are responsible for confirming that their Canvas time-zone settings are correct and for checking the exact deadline shown in Canvas well before an assessment closes.

  • Important changes from the original proposal.
  • Current blockers or team-health concerns.
  • Specific questions for the TA.
  • Planned work before the midterm report.

The update should be focused and should not repeat the entire proposal.

2. Individual Project-Wide Q&A #

The TA will direct questions to individual students. The student selected by the TA must answer the question without relying on another team member to answer first.

Questions may cover any part of the project, including:

  • Motivation and problem definition.
  • Data sources, preprocessing, and limitations.
  • Baselines, models, and technical choices.
  • Training and evaluation design.
  • Metrics and statistical interpretation.
  • Preliminary results and failure cases.
  • Code structure and reproducibility.
  • Team members’ contributions.
  • Responsible-AI considerations.
  • Generative-AI use.
  • Any paper, dataset, website, software package, or other source cited by the team.

The TA may select any cited source and ask any team member to explain:

  • The main idea or contribution of the source.
  • How the source relates to the project.
  • Which project claim or decision it supports.
  • Whether the team reproduced, modified, or rejected the source’s approach.

Every student is responsible for understanding the complete project, even when the team has divided the implementation work among its members.

Required Preparation #

Before the meeting, the team must provide the assigned TA with the following materials using the submission method announced by the instructional team:

  • Current project website or project summary page.
  • A working repository link accessible to the teaching team.
  • A concise progress summary of no more than one page.
  • A baseline result, preliminary output, or clear evidence of implementation progress.
  • An updated task and contribution table.
  • An updated Citation Verification Table.
  • A short list of focused questions for the TA, when applicable.
  • Private team-health information, if requested by the instructional team.

The repository, website, and supporting documents must be accessible before the meeting begins. Time lost because the team did not provide working access will reduce the amount of time available for discussion and Q&A.

Grading - 5 Course Points #

Criterion Scope Points
Evidence of meaningful progress, preparation, organization, and a realistic plan for the midterm report Team 2.0
Ability to answer individual project-wide questions accurately and demonstrate genuine understanding Individual 3.0
Total 5.0

The 2-point team score is normally shared by all team members.

The 3-point individual Q&A score is assigned separately to each student and may differ among members of the same team.

A student’s individual score may be reduced when the student:

  • Cannot explain important parts of the project.
  • Cannot explain the team’s data, baseline, model, evaluation, or preliminary results.
  • Cannot explain a cited source selected by the TA.
  • Relies on another member to answer.
  • Repeats memorized language without demonstrating understanding.
  • Cannot identify how a reported result was produced.
  • Gives answers that conflict with the submitted code, report, or experimental evidence.
  • Claims that the question concerns only another member’s work.

One incomplete answer does not automatically result in zero credit. The score will reflect the student’s overall demonstrated understanding during the meeting.

Forty-Eight-Hour Follow-Up Video #

When a student’s answer is incomplete or incorrect, the TA may, at the TA’s discretion, allow the student to submit one follow-up video.

The follow-up opportunity is not automatic and is not a substitute for preparing for the live meeting.

The TA will identify the specific questions or topics that the student must address. The video must:

  • Be submitted within 48 hours after the scheduled meeting.
  • Show the student on camera.
  • Answer the questions assigned by the TA.
  • Explain the answers in the student’s own words.
  • Demonstrate understanding rather than simply reading prepared text.
  • Be submitted privately through the method specified by the TA.

A follow-up video may recover up to 50% of the individual Q&A points lost during the meeting. It cannot provide more than full credit for the individual Q&A component.

Failure to submit the video within 48 hours means that the original individual Q&A score remains unchanged.

Attendance #

All team members are expected to attend the scheduled meeting.

A student with an officially approved exceptional circumstance will be given an alternative individual Q&A process determined by the teaching team. The student must follow the course’s Exceptional Circumstances policy.

A student who misses the meeting without an approved exception may receive zero for the 3-point individual Q&A component. An unapproved absence does not automatically qualify for a follow-up video or an individual rescheduled meeting.

Meeting Outcome #

At the end of the meeting, the TA may identify required corrections, action items, or questions that the team should address in the midterm project report.

The meeting is intended to provide direction and verify progress. It is not an approval of the final technical approach, a guarantee of a particular project grade, or a full code-debugging session.

Optional Advanced Project Bonus - Up to +5% #

The project bonus is added on top of the required 100% course grade.

  • Required coursework: 100%.
  • Maximum project bonus: +5 course percentage points.
  • Maximum possible final course score: 105%.

The bonus is intended to provide a meaningful cushion and reward teams that take their project beyond the complete required standard. It is optional. A strong project may earn full credit for the required 55% without earning bonus points.

A Strong Path to Bonus Credit: An Interactive ML/AI Platform #

One of the strongest and most practical ways to earn bonus credit is to transform the project into a working interactive ML or AI system.

Examples include an interactive website, software platform, visualization dashboard, decision-support tool, recommendation system, simulation environment, or deployed application that allows a user to:

  • Enter or upload new data.
  • Interact with the trained model.
  • Explore predictions or generated outputs.
  • Compare scenarios or model behavior.
  • Visualize results, uncertainty, explanations, or failure cases.
  • Receive a recommendation, ranking, warning, intervention, or next-best action.
  • Use the system to support a meaningful real-world decision or workflow.

Examples of suitable technologies include Streamlit, Gradio, Dash, GitHub Pages with an interactive backend, or another appropriate deployment framework.

A static website containing only the project report, figures, and presentation does not qualify as an advanced interactive system. Similarly, placing a basic interface around an existing API without meaningful technical work, evaluation, or analysis is not sufficient.

A strong interactive platform may support bonus requests under more than one category when each requested point represents a distinct and substantive contribution. For example, a team might separately demonstrate:

  • A technically challenging deployment.
  • A meaningful interactive visualization.
  • A decision-support capability.
  • A robustness or uncertainty analysis.
  • Evaluation with real users or domain experts.

The same feature may not be counted more than once.

How to Request Bonus Credit #

The final report, project website, and presentation must contain a clearly labeled Advanced Project Bonus section.

For each requested bonus point, the team must:

  • Identify the specific advanced contribution.
  • Identify the bonus category under which it is being requested.
  • Explain why it goes beyond the required project standard.
  • Provide direct evidence, such as code, experiments, results, a demonstration link, screenshots, or other artifacts.
  • Explain what the team learned from the contribution.
  • Identify the team members who made substantial contributions to it.

A required project element may not also be claimed as bonus work. Adding more pages, more models, more citations, or more code does not automatically qualify for bonus credit.

Bonus Categories #

Each category may earn up to +1 course percentage point, for a maximum total bonus of +5 course percentage points.

1. Advanced Technical Contribution - Up to +1% #

A technically significant extension beyond the normal course expectation, such as:

  • A carefully implemented advanced method.
  • A novel architecture or algorithmic modification.
  • A technically difficult model integration.
  • A meaningful improvement to an existing method.
  • A challenging real-time or large-scale system.
  • A nontrivial combination of multiple ML or AI components.

Using a larger model, calling a paid API, or changing a few hyperparameters without technical insight is not sufficient.

2. Exceptional Experimental Depth - Up to +1% #

A rigorous experimental analysis that materially improves the interpretation of the project, such as:

  • Ablation studies.
  • Sensitivity analysis.
  • Statistical significance or uncertainty analysis.
  • Calibration analysis.
  • Robustness testing.
  • Distribution-shift analysis.
  • Failure-mode analysis.
  • Careful comparison across datasets, populations, or operating conditions.

Running many models or reporting a large table of results without a clear experimental question is not sufficient.

3. Responsible, Secure, or Interpretable AI - Up to +1% #

A substantive and evidence-based analysis of an important responsible-AI issue, such as:

  • Fairness or bias.
  • Privacy.
  • Security or adversarial robustness.
  • Safety.
  • Explainability or interpretability.
  • Calibration and uncertainty.
  • Hallucination or factual reliability.
  • Human oversight.
  • Misuse risks.
  • Environmental or computational impact.

A short discussion paragraph without experiments, measurements, or a meaningful technical contribution is not sufficient.

4. Interactive ML System, Decision-Support Platform, or Meaningful Deployment - Up to +1% #

A working and usable interactive artifact that goes meaningfully beyond the required project website.

Strong examples include:

  • An interactive system that accepts new user input or uploaded data.
  • A visualization platform that allows users to explore model behavior dynamically.
  • A recommendation, ranking, prediction, or decision-support system.
  • A platform that suggests an action, intervention, warning, or next-best step.
  • A tool that allows users to compare multiple scenarios or model outputs.
  • A deployed application integrated into a meaningful workflow.
  • An interface that clearly communicates uncertainty, explanations, or limitations.

The artifact should be functional, accessible to the teaching team, and connected to the team’s actual models and experiments.

A static report website, a collection of screenshots, a nonfunctional mockup, or a simple interface around an existing API is not sufficient.

5. Original Data, External Evaluation, Reproducibility, or Open Contribution - Up to +1% #

An unusually strong contribution that creates value beyond the team’s final report, such as:

  • Responsible creation or annotation of an original dataset.
  • Meaningful evaluation with users, stakeholders, or domain experts.
  • Integration into a real external workflow.
  • An exceptionally complete reproducibility package.
  • A reusable open-source library, model, benchmark, or software component.
  • A substantial contribution accepted into an existing open-source project.
  • An unusually strong educational visualization or technical resource that others can use.

All privacy, licensing, human-subject, data-collection, and Georgia Tech requirements still apply.

Bonus Evaluation #

  • Bonus points are not guaranteed.
  • Partial bonus credit may be awarded.
  • The teaching team may verify any claimed contribution.
  • The teaching team must be able to access and test any claimed interactive system or deployed artifact.
  • A demonstration that works only in a prerecorded video may receive less credit than a working artifact accessible to the teaching team.
  • Any team member may be questioned about any bonus claim during the final presentation.
  • Students must understand the advanced work claimed by their team.
  • Bonus credit may be denied when the required project is incomplete, the evidence is weak, the contribution is misrepresented, or citation, reproducibility, privacy, or licensing requirements are not satisfied.
  • An impressive interface cannot compensate for an invalid model, unsupported claims, fabricated results, or a lack of meaningful evaluation.

Why Awards Matter #

Awards serve as a tangible recognition of the dedication and creativity students bring to their machine learning projects. Each semester, these select honors are chosen from among hundreds of submitted projects, making the competition fierce and underscoring the ingenuity and motivation of participating teams. By highlighting outstanding work, we encourage students to push boundaries, refine technical skills, and communicate findings more effectively.

This recognition also benefits students’ academic and professional growth—award recipients can feature their projects on resumes and link directly to showcased work for added visibility. Additionally, all projects remain live on the class website for future semesters, ensuring that both current and prospective students can discover, learn from, and be inspired by past successes. This tradition fosters continuity in the course and builds a growing body of shared knowledge. Being featured on the website is thus both an honor and a motivator, inspiring future cohorts to strive for the same high standard of excellence.
